# FIELD VISIT: DETAILED CONVERSATIONAL TRANSCRIPT SUMMARY
Date: 2026-05-02
Participants: Research Team (Interviewer), Main Staff Nurse, PHC Pharmacist.

---

## PART 1: INTERVIEW WITH THE MAIN STAFF NURSE (45 MINS)

The interview began with the Nurse explaining the core administrative structure. She emphasized that the Directorate of Public Health (DPH) and the DDHS (Deputy Director of Health Services) are the primary authorities they report to. She detailed how the PHC at Vellode and P.Kas operate as mid-level nodes, while the CHC in Chennimalai serves as the "Upgraded" center. When asked about the daily registration process, she explained the transition from HMIS 2.0 to 3.0. Currently, they have to categorize every patient as either "Old OP" or "New OP," but the portal is often slow in the mornings when the patient load is high.

Regarding Maternal Health, the Nurse spoke extensively about the PICME 2.0 system. She noted that while every Antenatal (AN) and Postnatal (PN) mother gets a unique 12-digit RCH ID, the system is mostly used for "data storage" rather than "real-time care." She highlighted a major bottleneck: if a high-risk mother is referred to the Chennimalai CHC for delivery, the PICME system doesn't automatically notify the receiving hospital. Instead, the nurse has to manually call the hospital or wait for the mother to bring back a paper slip. She admitted that because of this lack of a "Digital Handshake," they still maintain a physical delivery register to ensure no case is lost.

When the conversation moved to field work and the Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (MTM) program, she described the challenges faced by the Village Health Nurses (VHNs) at the Sub-centres (HSC). The VHNs are supposed to screen a population of approximately 6000 for NCDs (Non-Communicable Diseases). However, in remote villages, there is often zero internet connectivity. The nurses have to record everything in a field diary and then come back to the PHC or go home to enter the data into the MTM portal during their personal time. She also mentioned the "MTM School" program where they screen students for health defects, but again, the reporting is delayed because it relies on the same slow portal.

On the topic of staff attendance and management, she discussed the FRAS (Face Recognition Attendance System). While it was meant to be a modern solution, she pointed out that the face scanner often fails to recognize staff in the early morning or if the lighting is poor. In such cases, they have to resort back to the handwritten log book. Similarly, for leave and salary, they use the Kalanjiyam portal, but she expressed frustration that for emergency leave, the portal is too slow. The DDHS cannot see a "Live Map" of which PHCs are currently understaffed, which is a major administrative gap.

Finally, she touched upon the sensitive area of "Social Referrals." This includes tracking school dropouts, teenage pregnancies, and unmarried pregnancies. She explained that because of the high social stigma, these records are kept in a "Secret Diary" and not entered into the main HMIS or PICME systems to protect the privacy of the girls. She suggested that a secure, encrypted digital module for these sensitive cases would be a huge "Upgrade" for the DDHS to monitor Maternal Mortality Rates (MMR) more effectively.

## PART 2: INTERVIEW WITH THE PHC PHARMACIST (45 MINS)

The Pharmacist's interview focused heavily on the logistics and the HMIS reporting structure. He explained that the "Monthly Report" for the DDHS is a massive task that covers 13 different departments including Surgery, Medicine, OBGYN, and Paediatrics. Every month, he and the team have to spend 2-3 days manually calculating the totals from the "Daily Diary" to enter them into the HMIS portal. He emphasized that if the system were "Real-Time," this work would be completely eliminated.

When asked about medicine stock and inventory, he explained the "Bin Card" system. Every medicine box in the pharmacy has a physical paper card (Bin Card) where they manually subtract the quantity every time a medicine is issued. While the "Newer" drugs are supposed to be tracked in HMIS 2.0, the "General Supplies" (like bandages, syringes, and common pills) are often not updated in the system daily. This leads to "Sudden Stock-outs" where a PHC might run out of paracetamol, but the DDHS doesn't find out until the next monthly report is filed.

The discussion then turned to Biomedical Waste Management. He confirmed that the "Ramky" agency is responsible for collecting the waste segregated into the 5 color-coded bags. However, the communication with Ramky is entirely paper-based. The driver provides a handwritten receipt, and the pharmacist files it. If the waste collection is missed for a few days, there is no automated alert to the DDHS; the pharmacist just has to keep calling the agency.

The Pharmacist also highlighted the challenges with the ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account) ID registration. While the government wants every patient to have a digital health ID, most of the elderly patients in the village do not have their mobile numbers linked to their Aadhar cards. This makes the OTP-based registration impossible. He suggested that the system needs a workaround for rural populations, such as fingerprint-based ABHA generation.

He concluded by discussing the "HMIS 3.0" transition. He hopes that the new version will solve some of the connectivity and data-loading issues, but he remains skeptical because the underlying problem of "Duplicate Entry" (writing in a book and then typing in a computer) has not yet been addressed. He strongly feels that a "Unified Command Center" for the DDHS would be the biggest improvement for district health management.
